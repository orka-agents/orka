Task webhooks

Set spec.webhookURL on a Task and the controller POSTs a JSON notice when the Task reaches a terminal phase (Succeeded, Failed, or Cancelled). Every notice is signed with an Ed25519 key. Delivery is durable across controller restarts, retried with bounded backoff, and limited to destinations that are safe for a controller to call.

apiVersion: core.orka.ai/v1alpha1
kind: Task
metadata:
  name: nightly-report
spec:
  type: ai
  prompt: "Summarize yesterday's incidents"
  webhookURL: https://hooks.example.com/orka

Where a notice may go​

The controller fails closed. A destination must be one of:

  • A public http or https host. The name is resolved when the controller connects, on every connection, and every address it resolves to must be globally routable. Loopback, RFC 1918, CGNAT (100.64.0.0/10), link-local (including 169.254.169.254), IPv6 unique-local and link-local, multicast, broadcast, unspecified (0.0.0.0, ::), IPv4-mapped forms of any of these, and the other IANA special-use ranges are refused. A DNS failure is a failed attempt, never a pass, and because the check runs at connect time a name that is re-pointed after validation (DNS rebinding) cannot reach an internal address.
  • A Service in the Task's own namespace, as http://<name>.<namespace>.svc[.cluster.local][:port]/.... The Service must exist, have a selector and a ClusterIP, and not be ExternalName. The controller connects straight to that ClusterIP without DNS. Services in other namespaces and kubernetes.default are refused.

The controller never follows redirects (a 3xx is a failed attempt) and never sends webhooks through an HTTP proxy, even when HTTP_PROXY is set, because a proxy would connect on its behalf and skip these checks. If your controller needs an egress proxy, put a same-namespace Service in front of it.

A URL with a non-http(s) scheme, a literal non-public IP, a blocked host name, or another namespace's Service is rejected without any network attempt: the delivery goes straight to Failed.

The request​

POST /orka HTTP/1.1
Content-Type: application/json
User-Agent: Orka-Controller/1.0
X-Orka-Task: nightly-report
X-Orka-Namespace: team-a
X-Orka-Event: task.succeeded
X-Orka-Delivery-ID: 6f1c3c2e-5b0a-4f7e-9a51-0d3f1f7f6a10
Idempotency-Key: 6f1c3c2e-5b0a-4f7e-9a51-0d3f1f7f6a10
X-Orka-Delivery-Attempt: 1
X-Orka-Signature: eyJhbGciOiJFZERTQSIsImtpZCI6Ii4uLiIsInR5cCI6IkpXVCJ9...

{"deliveryId":"6f1c3c2e-...","eventType":"task.succeeded","occurredAt":"2026-11-06T10:00:00Z",
 "taskUID":"0b9e0f43-...","taskName":"nightly-report","taskNamespace":"team-a",
 "phase":"Succeeded","message":"...","startTime":"...","completionTime":"...",
 "attempts":1,"resultRef":{"available":true}}

eventType is task.succeeded, task.failed, or task.cancelled. Every attempt of one delivery sends a byte-identical body under the same delivery ID; only X-Orka-Delivery-Attempt and the signature change.

Any 2xx response acknowledges the notice. Respond quickly; one attempt times out after 30 seconds.

Verifying a notice​

X-Orka-Signature is a compact JWS (a JWT) signed with alg: EdDSA (Ed25519). Its header carries the signing key's kid, the RFC 7638 thumbprint of the public key. The public keys are published, without authentication, by the Orka API:

GET https://<orka-api>/.well-known/orka/task-webhook-jwks.json
{"keys":[{"kty":"OKP","crv":"Ed25519","x":"...","kid":"...","alg":"EdDSA","use":"sig"}]}

Fetch the JWKS out of band or over a trusted channel: it is what your receiver trusts. Then, for each request:

  1. Read kid from the JWS header and find that key in your cached JWKS. If it is missing, refetch the JWKS once (rate-limit this) and try again; reject if it is still missing. Accept only alg: EdDSA.
  2. Verify the Ed25519 signature over the first two dot-separated segments.
  3. Check the claims:
    • iss is orka.ai/task-webhook;
    • aud is exactly the webhookURL configured on the Task;
    • exp is in the future and nbf is not, allowing about a minute of clock skew. Tokens live for five minutes and are minted for each attempt;
    • payload_sha256 equals the lowercase hex SHA-256 of the raw request body you received, before any JSON parsing.
  4. Use task.uid, task.namespace, task.name, event, and delivery_id from the verified claims rather than from headers or the body.
  5. Deduplicate on delivery_id (also sent as X-Orka-Delivery-ID and Idempotency-Key). Delivery is at-least-once: a controller that restarts mid-attempt sends the same delivery again.

The claim set looks like:

{
  "iss": "orka.ai/task-webhook",
  "sub": "0b9e0f43-...",
  "aud": "https://hooks.example.com/orka",
  "iat": 1793872800, "nbf": 1793872800, "exp": 1793873100,
  "jti": "6f1c3c2e-....1",
  "delivery_id": "6f1c3c2e-...",
  "attempt": 1,
  "event": "task.succeeded",
  "task": {"uid": "0b9e0f43-...", "namespace": "team-a", "name": "nightly-report"},
  "payload_sha256": "9f86d081884c7d659a2feaa0c55ad015a3bf4f1b2b0b822cd15d6c15b0f00a08"
}

Any standard JOSE library that supports EdDSA can do steps 1–3. In Go, github.com/lestrrat-go/jwx/v3 verifies with jwt.ParseString(token, jwt.WithKeySet(set), jwt.WithIssuer("orka.ai/task-webhook"), jwt.WithAudience(webhookURL)) followed by the payload_sha256 comparison. Orka's own reference implementation is taskwebhook.Verify in internal/taskwebhook.

Retries and status​

The delivery is recorded in the Task's status, so it survives controller restarts, upgrades, and leader changes:

status:
  webhookDelivered: true
  webhookDelivery:
    deliveryID: 6f1c3c2e-5b0a-4f7e-9a51-0d3f1f7f6a10
    eventType: task.succeeded
    occurredAt: "2026-11-06T10:00:00Z"
    payloadSHA256: 9f86d0...
    state: Delivered          # Pending, Delivered, or Failed
    attempts: 1
    lastStatusCode: 202
  • A failed attempt (connection error, timeout, refused address, redirect, or a non-2xx response) is retried after 10s, 20s, 40s, and so on, capped at 15 minutes, for at most 8 attempts. After that the delivery is Failed and is never retried. lastError records a short reason with no URL or credential in it.
  • Each attempt is claimed in Task status before it is sent. A controller that dies mid-attempt leaves a two-minute lease; the next controller waits for it, then retries under the same delivery ID.
  • Only one notice is pending per Task. If the Task's terminal state changes before a pending notice is delivered, the controller supersedes it: the old delivery ID is never sent again, a new one carries the newer state, its attempts start over, and superseded counts the replaced notices. Supersession never shortens an existing backoff.
  • If signing is enabled but no signing key is available yet (for example, the Secret cannot be read or holds a malformed key), nothing is sent and no attempt is consumed; the delivery stays Pending until a key is available.
  • If signing is disabled (--task-webhook-signing-secret=""), nothing is sent and the delivery is marked Failed at once. Enabling signing later does not retry notices that already failed this way.

Signing keys and rotation​

The controller signs with the Ed25519 key in the current item of a Secret in a namespace of its own: <release namespace>-task-webhook-signing for the Helm chart (override with controller.taskWebhooks.signingNamespace), or orka-task-webhook-signing for the kustomize install (flag --task-webhook-signing-namespace). The Secret is <fullname>-task-webhook-signing for the Helm chart, where <fullname> is the chart's full name (orka for a release named orka; override the Secret name with controller.taskWebhooks.signingSecretName), or orka-task-webhook-signing for the kustomize install. On first use, the controller creates that Secret and mints current if either is missing. Keys are PEM-encoded PKCS#8, which is what openssl genpkey -algorithm ed25519 writes. A malformed key is never replaced: delivery pauses until it is fixed.

Every replica rereads the Secret at least every 15 seconds, so rotation needs no restart. To rotate:

NS=orka-task-webhook-signing      # <release namespace>-task-webhook-signing for Helm
SECRET=orka-task-webhook-signing  # <fullname>-task-webhook-signing for Helm
openssl genpkey -algorithm ed25519 -out new.pem
OLD=$(kubectl -n "$NS" get secret "$SECRET" -o jsonpath='{.data.current}')
kubectl -n "$NS" patch secret "$SECRET" --type merge \
  -p "{\"data\":{\"previous\":\"$OLD\",\"current\":\"$(base64 -w0 < new.pem)\"}}"
shred -u new.pem

The new key signs from then on. The JWKS publishes both current and previous, so a token signed by a replica that has not yet reloaded still verifies, and receivers that see the new kid refetch the JWKS. After TokenTTL plus the reload interval (in practice, wait at least ten minutes), drop previous:

kubectl -n "$NS" patch secret "$SECRET" --type json -p '[{"op":"remove","path":"/data/previous"}]'

If a key is compromised, rotate and remove previous at once. Receivers that keep the old JWKS cached keep trusting the old key until they refetch.

Setting --task-webhook-signing-secret="" disables signing. Since notices are never sent unsigned, that also stops webhook delivery: notices for Tasks that finish while signing is disabled are marked Failed and are not retried after signing is enabled again.

Who can read the signing key​

Anyone who can read or change the signing Secret can forge notices, so it lives alone in its own namespace and only the controller is bound there: get and update on that one Secret, and create on Secrets there. This closes two paths:

  • A Task's or Agent's secretRef mounts a Secret from the Task's namespace. Tasks live in the release namespace, so a secretRef cannot name the key.
  • Worker roles are bound in the release namespace only, so no worker can read or replace the key with its own permissions.

The protection also depends on no worker being able to act as the controller. The default AI worker RBAC profile lets the AI worker create Secrets and Jobs in the release namespace (for code_exec and child Task credentials). A ServiceAccount-token Secret or a Job could name the controller's ServiceAccount, and a Job's Pod could carry the controller's labels, which would put it behind the API Service that publishes the JWKS. The <fullname>-ai-worker-identity-protection ValidatingAdmissionPolicy, which both installs ship, blocks all three. The AI worker may only write Opaque Secrets, may only create or delete code_exec's own ServiceAccounts (so it cannot replace a platform account and inherit its RoleBindings), and may only create code_exec Jobs: each runs as its own per-run ServiceAccount with no token mounted, carries only code_exec labels, uses only emptyDir and Secret volumes, gets no host namespaces, privilege or capabilities, and runs under a RuntimeDefault or Localhost seccomp profile. Keep that policy installed (Helm workers.ai.identityProtection defaults to true; turning it off reopens the path to the controller's identity under the default worker profile), and:

  • Do not grant the AI worker other ways to run Pods or request tokens. An OutboundAccessPolicy serviceAccountRef grants the AI worker TokenRequest for that account, so the controller refuses Orka's own ServiceAccounts there: any labeled orka.ai/platform-identity: "true", plus the controller and worker accounts it is configured with, labeled or not. The AI worker's own account is allowed, since a token for itself gives it nothing new.
  • Do not keep ServiceAccount-token Secrets in the release namespace. The default AI worker profile can read every Secret there, so any such Secret hands the worker that account's token.

The controller refuses to start with a signing namespace that is the watch, controller Pod, ACP runtime or agent-host namespace. The chart also refuses the client namespace when rendering; the controller cannot see that one, so kustomize and other installs must keep it apart themselves. Keep the signing namespace free of other workloads and of role bindings beyond the controller's and your cluster administrators'.
